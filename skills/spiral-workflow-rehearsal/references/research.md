# Research basis and limits

Status: research-informed design synthesis, not a validated method. These primary sources supply analytical distinctions; none establishes that language-model role rehearsal predicts people or discovers all failures. Spiral's records, review sequence and reporting format are proposed design choices.

| Primary source | Basis and Spiral adaptation | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Hollan, Hutchins and Kirsh (2000), *Distributed Cognition*, ACM TOCHI 7(2), 174–196. [Author-hosted final revision](https://hci.ucsd.edu/hollan/Pubs/tochi-revised.pdf) | Analyze people, representations, tools and time together. Trace facts through storage, transformation, visibility and use; include artifacts retaining context across handoffs. | Foundational theoretical framework, not evidence that simulated actors substitute for people. Do not infer mental states. |
| Clark and Brennan (1991), *Grounding in Communication*, 127–149. [Author-hosted chapter](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Clark%2C%20H.H.%20_%20Brennan%2C%20S.E.%20_Grounding%20in%20communication_%201991.pdf) | Presenting information differs from establishing adequate evidence of understanding; media impose coordination costs. Separate availability from evidence of understanding or responsibility acceptance. | Conceptual treatment, not a mandatory sequence of UI statuses. A receipt or click is only design-defined evidence, not proof of understanding. Do not require acknowledgment for every low-stakes event. |
| Tam and Greenberg (2006), *A Framework for Asynchronous Change Awareness in Collaborative Documents and Workspaces*, IJHCS 64(7), 583–598. [Author-lab paper](https://grouplab.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/grouplab/uploads/Publications/Publications/2006-ChangeAwareness.IJHCS.pdf) | Examine who changed what, where, when, how and why from artifact, person and workspace perspectives. Use a returning actor's last-seen checkpoint to assess relevant intervening changes. | Framework demonstrated through design critique, explicitly not formally validated. Conflicts and ownership handoffs are outside its scope; Spiral's coverage is additional synthesis. More history can overwhelm. |
| Malone and Crowston (1994), *The Interdisciplinary Study of Coordination*, ACM Computing Surveys 26(1), 87–119. [Author-hosted paper](https://crowston.syr.edu/sites/default/files/acmcs94.pdf) | Characterize dependencies among activities, including shared resources, producer/consumer, simultaneity and task/subtask relations. Name whether a wait needs input, allocation or sequencing. | Cross-disciplinary synthesis, not a universal process or scoring system. A shared queue is not an ownership policy; dependency maps do not establish organizational incentives. |
| Russell, van der Aalst and ter Hofstede (2006), *Workflow Exception Patterns*. [Author-hosted paper](https://www.vdaalst.com/publications/p325.pdf) | Distinguish work-item failure, deadline expiry, unavailable resources, external triggers and constraint violations. Separate local response, case-wide effects and recovery ownership. | Classification applied to eight workflow offerings. Its model assumes identifiable, immediately detectable exceptions; Spiral must challenge this with detection-gap checks. Not exhaustive or human-rehearsal validation. |
| OMG (2013), *BPMN 2.0.2*, especially clauses 13.3–13.5. [Official specification](https://www.omg.org/spec/BPMN/2.0.2/PDF/) | Explicit transitions, branches, events and compensation distinguish stopping active work from handling completed effects. Use a small state sketch when prose obscures these. | Authoritative notation, not empirical usability evidence. Spiral sketches are not BPMN conformance, executable simulation or formal verification; full BPMN is optional and outside default scope. |

Use the relevant lens only. These distinctions can strengthen a source-linked review, but cannot turn assumed events into observations or grant actors knowledge they never received. See [conditional cases](conditional-cases.md) for applications and unexecuted validation ideas.
